# Fourth Progress Report — English Script

This script follows all 12 slides in [Group01-4.pptx](Group01-4.pptx). Allow about five minutes, including a short pause for the loop on slide 9.

## Slide 1 — Cover (8 s)

Good morning. We’re Group One. This is our fourth progress report on the block-clearing robot.

## Slide 2 — Contents (10 s)

We’ll show the new frame and how we use the camera to control the robot. Then we’ll cover the problems we found and what we’ll test next.

## Slide 3 — Frame Assembly (31 s)

On the left is the CAD drawing from our third report. In the centre is the frame now fitted to the robot. It is 3D printed and measures 150 by 100 by 170 millimetres. The motors and drivers sit on the bottom deck, and the battery and ESP32 sit in the middle. The top deck holds the ArUco tag. The photo on the right shows a guard that helps keep blocks out of the wheel opening.

## Slide 4 — Continuous Wheel-Speed Loop (26 s)

The photo shows the wiring. The ESP32 sets a base motor output and uses PI control to correct the difference between target and actual wheel speed. The encoders give us actual speed. The controller updates every 10 milliseconds, with PWM capped at 80 percent in speed mode. A speed command lasts at most 150 milliseconds. The robot stops if it expires or feedback fails.

## Slide 5 — Field Mapping and Robot Position (27 s)

The photo shows the corrected overhead image with a calibration grid. We use four field corners to match image points to positions on the field. The corners of the 50-millimetre ArUco tag, ID Seven, tell us where the robot is and which way it faces. The floor colour around each red block tells us which side it is on.

## Slide 6 — From Camera to Motors (27 s)

The overhead phone sends video to Windows over USB using scrcpy. Windows finds the robot and blocks. The planner works out how the robot should move forward, sideways or turn, then sends those commands to the ESP32 over Bluetooth SPP. Before it moves, the software checks the field calibration, whether the position and heading are up to date, and whether the robot is ready.

## Slide 7 — Motion Calibration on the Robot (34 s)

The loop shows the first few calibration moves. We recorded the robot’s position, heading and encoder counts while it was still. We repeated six moves three times each, completing 18 segments. That gave 21.61 counts per millimetre forward or backward, 25.63 sideways, and 37.84 counts per degree of turn. In an early test, we sent STOP at a reading of 137.3 millimetres. The robot travelled 254.9 millimetres in total. We lowered the target from 5,963 to 1,987 counts per second.

## Slide 8 — Live View During Manual Control (23 s)

The keyboard preview uses the same image-processing code and field calibration as the automatic task. It once went black because the image had the wrong rotation. We set it to 90 degrees to match the saved calibration. The preview updates separately from the keyboard controls. Offline tests with simulated images checked the display and whether closing it stopped the camera feed.

## Slide 9 — Recorded Keyboard Test (39 s)

Here the robot is driven from the keyboard. The screen marks the divider, the robot’s tag and the red blocks. The opening frame shows an age of 109 milliseconds, measured from when it was decoded on the computer. The software briefly loses the tag near the edge of the image.

*Give the audience about 15 seconds to watch the loop, then advance.*

## Slide 10 — Field Issues, Changes and Tests (38 s)

First, Bluetooth timed out between calibration moves. PING messages now go out on their own schedule, even when we stop the robot or read the encoders. Offline tests checked the timing. Second, floor glare was mistaken for an obstacle. On one saved frame, the filter brought false detections down from one to zero. Third, the task reported missing wheel feedback. The new firmware reports the wheel, target speed, measured speed and PWM when a fault stops the robot. It compiled successfully and still needs to be installed and tested on the robot.

## Slide 11 — Tests on the Robot (29 s)

We’ll install the new firmware and repeat the turn test, comparing target and measured speeds for each wheel. Next, we’ll push one block across the line and record the robot’s position, heading, wheel commands and when it stops. Then we’ll test a full automatic run and log changes in the task, any loss of camera tracking, and the reasons for stops. We’ll also retest detection of the other robot on the field.

## Slide 12 — Thank You (8 s)

That concludes our fourth progress report. Thank you.
